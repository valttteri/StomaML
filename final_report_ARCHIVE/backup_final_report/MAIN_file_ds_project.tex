	\section{Introduction}\label{intro}
	
Stomatal sizes, shapes and densities fluctuate by environmental factors \autocite{hetheringtonRoleStomataSensing2003, cassonInfluenceEnvironmentalFactors2008, bertolinoImpactStomatalDensity2019}. Analysing stomatal characteristics is crucial for understanding plant responses to environmental and climate changes \autocite{hetheringtonRoleStomataSensing2003, yanContrastingResponsesLeaf2017, liangStomatalResponsesTerrestrial2023}.

Several attempts have been made to automatate stomatal detection with computer vision since the 1980s \autocite{omasaMeasurementStomatalAperture1984,karabourniotisEpicuticularPhenolicsGuard2001,lagaImagebasedPlantStornata2014,duarteSegmentingHighqualityDigital2017}. Recently, deep learning approaches have improved the accuracy and made the models more general \autocite{fetterStomataCounterNeuralNetwork2019,saiStomaAIEfficientUserfriendly2023,gibbsDeepLearningMethod2021}. Still, the tools tend to be species specific, do not maintain acceptable accuracy for species they have not been trained with or contain only limited morphological traits \autocite{gibbsApplicationDeepLearning2024}. 

Images of birch leaves cannot currently be handled automatically with existing solutions. Instead, the stomata for birch tree leaves must be counted manually which is extremely time consuming and forms a significant bottleneck in research workflows \autocite{saiStomaAIEfficientUserfriendly2023, gibbsApplicationDeepLearning2024}. This is because the topography of birch leaf surfaces are uneven, making parts of the images blurry even if one would try to stack multiple images. In addition, some leaf structures, like veins or trichomes cannot contain stomata, and should be excluded from a valid countable area \autocite{valkamaComparativeAnalysisLeaf2003}. In order to be able to identify stomata and achieve a meaningful stomatal density, a targeted solution for birch leaves is needed.

We have developed a standalone web application, StomaML, that automatically detects and quantifies stomata in images of birch leaves. Users can upload batches of leaf images, and the system outputs a CSV file containing stomata counts per image, countable area of stomata (excluding veins and artifacts), stomatal density \& stomata size measurements, as well as comprehensive metadata about each object identified per image. With automation the analysis of tens of images could be done quickly, and a subsequent correlation study would be made significantly easier.

	\section{Technical approach}\label{methods}

	We used Roboflow for annotating and training our computer vision models \autocite{dwyer2024roboflow}. Roboflow makes the tedious annotation somewhat more tolerable: There is a possibility to add and manage multiple object classes, a smart selection tool that runs on SAM 3 \autocite{carionSAM3Segment2025}, and convenient way of assigning images for annotation among project members. After annotating the first 100 images, we used the existing models to pre-annote the rest of the pictures. Still the annotation took a large share of our total time with the project. Luckily, the  client could mobilize five people to help us annotate the non-countable area and do sanity checks for the stomatal detection.

	Roboflow also makes it convenient to train and test the models. The model could be exported to deployment directly from roboflow.
